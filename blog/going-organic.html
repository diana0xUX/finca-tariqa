What it actually takes to go organic — and why we're doing it on an 80-million-year-old hillside

The bureaucratic reality behind organic certification in Valencia, the loophole that might shorten our three-year wait, and what ancient limestone has to do with any of it.

Organic CAECV By Diana Voroneca 8 min read

Last week I sent two WhatsApp messages that might change how this farm runs for the next twenty years. One to the cooperative. One to the man we bought the land from. Both of them asked the same thing: what, exactly, was sprayed on these orange trees before we arrived?

That question — boring on its surface, significant underneath — is the beginning of what it actually means to go organic. Not the values part. The paperwork part. The three-year-clock-that-might-already-be-ticking part.

Here's what we've learned.

· · ·

First: why this land makes organic a different argument

Most organic conversion stories start with a values statement. Ours starts 80 million years ago, at the bottom of a warm shallow sea.

The rock under the orange grove is Upper Cretaceous limestone — the same formation that runs through the spine of Ribera Alta, the same geology that underlies some of the most minerally expressive wines in Europe. It is porous, fractured, slightly alkaline, and hostile in ways that turn out to be useful.

The Burgundy maxim — "the vine must suffer to produce great wine" — is not mysticism. It is terpene chemistry observed empirically over centuries. The same physics applies here.

The limestone hillside does something that flat valley floor farms cannot replicate: it applies mild, chronic water stress to the trees through summer. The result is fruit with higher sugar concentration and a more complex aromatic profile — the same principle behind regulated deficit irrigation, a scientifically validated technique where growers deliberately restrict water to raise quality. Here the hillside does it for free, every season.

A 2026 study found that water deficit triggers a spike in limonene — the dominant aroma compound in orange peel — and an increase in stress-signalling aromatic aldehydes. The tree makes perfume as a stress response. Our hillside has been running this process continuously for fifty years, across 1,100 trees, without anyone positioning it as a quality argument. That's the marketing gap organic certification would open.

Nobody is selling the terroir of limestone hillside oranges as distinct from valley floor production. That story doesn't exist yet. The chemistry supports it.

Organic, on this land, is not an ideological choice. It is the agronomic direction the limestone is already pointing toward.

· · ·

What organic actually means, legally

In EU law, "organic" is not a philosophy. It is a clock.

Under Regulation EU 2018/848, any farm converting to organic must complete a mandatory conversion period before its first fruit can carry the EU organic leaf logo. For perennial crops — which includes all citrus — that period is three years from the day you stop using prohibited inputs and register with a certified control body.

During those three years, you manage the land under full organic rules: no synthetic pesticides, no synthetic fertilisers, no herbicides. The fruit from those years is labelled "en conversión a la agricultura ecológica" — in conversion — and sold at conventional or near-conventional prices. You run organic costs. You earn conventional revenue. Then, in year four, you get the certificate.

That is the standard path. There is another one.

This is why we messaged the previous owner. If he can confirm no prohibited treatments were applied, the three-year clock may have already run. The single most valuable WhatsApp we've ever sent.

The retroactive provision exists because the EU understood that some land has effectively been organic for years without the paperwork to prove it. Small family-farmed plots in Spain often receive minimal chemical inputs simply because the economics of applying them on difficult hillside terrain don't make sense. The land may already qualify. The question is whether the evidence exists to demonstrate it.

That evidence is what we're waiting for now. If the previous owner confirms no synthetic inputs — and if the cooperative's records say the same — we apply for retroactive recognition, pay the fee, take the inspector's visit, and skip most of the conversion wait.

The certification body: CAECV

Organic certification in Valencia runs through the CAECV — Comité de Agricultura Ecológica de la Comunitat Valenciana. They inspect and certify under EU Reg. 2018/848, issuing the ES-ECO-020-CV mark. Their office covering Carcaixent is in Carlet, twenty minutes away.

What it costs

One thing organic literature rarely makes clear: the certification costs are small. The income risk during conversion — selling organic-managed fruit at conventional prices — is the actual financial question. The certification itself is a few hundred euros a year.

The GVA subsidy — Orden 6/2024 — covers up to 80% of annual maintenance fees for certified organic operators. The 2026 window has already closed, but it runs annually. Once we're registered, the effective annual cost is around €56. The registration is a one-time €312.

By comparison: a bottle of certified organic Valencia oranges sold direct to a consumer in northern Europe retails for two to three times the cooperative bulk price. The certification cost pays for itself within the first kilo of premium-priced fruit.

The cooperative question

Here is the part that actually determines whether organic conversion makes financial sense: the cooperative.

Certification earns you the right to call your fruit organic. But someone has to buy it at organic prices. Exagro — the cooperative currently handling this grove — is GlobalG.A.P. and BRC certified. Conventional. If transitional fruit goes to Exagro, it earns conventional prices during the three-year conversion. The organic clock runs. The premium doesn't materialise yet.

Cooperativa La Casella, based in the valley immediately below this property, runs a dedicated Naranjas Eco line. If they accept transitional fruit — and sell it at an organic premium — the conversion period becomes revenue-generating rather than revenue-neutral. This changes the entire financial calculation.

We've asked Exagro directly. We're waiting for the answer. That single reply will tell us whether the transition is a three-year cost or a three-year opportunity.

⚠ What we're waiting on: Two WhatsApp replies — the previous owner's confirmation that no synthetic inputs were applied, and the cooperative's policy on transitional fruit. Those two answers define the path forward entirely.
· · ·

What we can start today, without waiting for anything

Three practices advance the organic timeline and improve the grove agronomically, starting this season, without certification, without cooperative permission, and without reducing conventional income.

Cover crops. After Lane-Late harvest in June, sow hairy vetch and a grass mix between the terrace rows. Mow instead of spray. Leave the cut material as mulch. This rebuilds the soil biology that intensive management suppresses — and it is compatible with current GlobalG.A.P. certification. Start this October.

Mulch under trees. Shred pruning waste with a chipper. Spread 5–8 cm deep under the drip line of every tree, keeping 20 cm clear of the trunk. This retains moisture, suppresses weeds mechanically, and feeds the soil biology. One of the most effective single interventions on drip-irrigated limestone grove.

Stop herbicide sprays. Replace chemical weed control with mowing. This is the most visible shift in management and the one that matters most for the organic application — it is the clearest evidence that the conversion period has begun.

✓ Everything that benefits the grove agronomically also advances the organic timeline. These practices are not in tension with production. Start them for agronomic reasons. The certificate follows. The IVIA research on this is unambiguous: humic acids and vermicompost have been shown to increase leaf iron content by 57% in year one and 103% in year two — addressing the iron chlorosis problem this limestone soil creates while simultaneously building the soil biology organic certification requires.
· · ·

And then: carbon

There is a chapter after organic certification that we are starting to document now, because it requires a baseline that can only be established at the beginning.

A 13-year study on regenerative orange grove management on calcareous Mediterranean soil found that organic management turned a conventional grove from a net carbon emitter into a measurable net carbon sink. The EU Carbon Removal Certification Framework — Regulation EU 2024/3012, adopted December 2024 — is the first EU-wide framework for selling that stored carbon to companies that need to offset emissions. It becomes fully operational around 2028–2030.

A grove that establishes soil carbon baseline records now — five soil cores, €300–500 at an IVIA-accredited lab — has five years of documented improvement to monetise when the market opens. A grove that starts in 2030 starts with nothing.

This is why the organic question, the cooperative question, and the soil question are all connected. The certification is not an endpoint. It is the paperwork that makes everything else legible — to buyers, to markets, to the carbon accounting systems that will matter more, not less, over the next decade.

· · ·

The trees here are fifty years old. They predate the cooperative. They predate the current irrigation system. They certainly predate the CAECV's certification procedures.

What they have been doing for fifty years — on thin limestone soil, under mild stress, producing concentrated, aromatic fruit above a valley floor that produces perfectly adequate conventional citrus — is more interesting than anyone has so far described.

We're going to try to describe it. The paperwork is step one.

We'll update this post when we hear back from the cooperative and the previous owner. The CAECV call is this week — 96 253 82 41, mornings before noon.
