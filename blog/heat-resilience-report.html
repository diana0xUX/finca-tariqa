What Happens to an Orange Tree at 42°C

Spain has just recorded its hottest summer in history. Again. A practical investigation into what extreme heat does to a citrus grove, a rural home, and a family — and what we're doing about it.

Finca Tariqa · Carcaixent, Valencia  ·  August 2026

This summer, Spain crossed a threshold it had already crossed last year. The hottest summer in recorded history — again. AEMET issued red alerts for Valencia in July. In June alone, health authorities estimated over a thousand excess deaths nationally from heat. The Ministerio de Sanidad activated its national heat emergency plan on the 16th of May, earlier than it ever has before.

We have a baby. We have 1,100 orange trees. Both of them need us to figure this out.

So over the past weeks, we've been researching everything we can about what heat does to citrus orchards and old Mediterranean farmhouses — and what people in similar situations have actually done about it. This report is what we found.

It is written for ourselves first — a thinking document, a plan. But we're publishing it because we suspect we're not the only small farm on a limestone hillside trying to figure this out without a university agronomy department on speed dial.

What heat actually does to an orange tree

An orange tree is a Mediterranean plant. It evolved for dry heat and long summers. But it evolved for the heat of the 20th century, not this one.

At around 35°C, citrus begins to show stress. Stomata — the tiny pores on the underside of leaves — start to close. The tree is doing what any living thing does when it's losing water faster than it can replace it: it tries to seal itself. The problem is that stomata also let in CO₂, which is how the tree feeds itself. A tree with closed stomata is a tree that has stopped photosynthesising. It's not dying yet. But it's not growing, either.

At 38°C, sustained over two or more consecutive days, fruit begins to drop. The tree performs a kind of triage — shedding fruit it can no longer support so it can protect its own tissues. In a bad year, this can mean losing 20–30% of the harvest to nothing more than air temperature.

At 42°C — which this region recorded multiple times this July — the outer skin of the fruit, still on the tree, can sunburn. Not metaphorically. The surface exposed to direct solar radiation becomes damaged at the cellular level. The fruit looks scorched. It isn't saleable.

And beneath all of this, the soil. On an unshaded, unmulched grove surface during a 40°C day, the soil temperature can reach 55–60°C. At that temperature, root activity in the top 15 centimetres — where most of the feeder roots live — effectively shuts down. The tree is being cooked from both ends.

This is the situation we are farming into. Not in the future. Now.

Case Study 1 — The White Spray
Murcia, Spain · Organic lemon grove · 2022–present

How a mineral clay older than farming became a heat shield

In 2022, a third-generation family citrus farm in Murcia was losing close to 30% of their lemon crop each year to sunburn — damaged fruit on the tree that couldn't be sold. They were organic certified and couldn't use the synthetic antitranspirant sprays some conventional farms use. A technician from their organic certification body suggested something that sounded almost primitive: spray the trees with white clay.

Kaolin clay — the same fine white mineral used in porcelain and cosmetics — dries into a reflective film on leaves and fruit. It bounces solar radiation away before it reaches the plant tissue. It has no chemical action whatsoever. It is, literally, making the tree white enough to reflect the sun.

The farm began applying a 3% kaolin suspension (30 grams per litre of water) every three to four weeks from late May through September. The results over the first full season were clear enough that they've done it every year since: sunburn losses fell from 30% to under 5% of crop. Irrigation demand dropped by an estimated 15% because leaf surface temperature was lower and the trees were losing less water through transpiration. And — unexpected bonus — they noticed fewer infestations of the African citrus psyllid, an invasive pest whose adults don't like landing on pale, chalky surfaces.

The total cost of kaolin for their six-hectare grove: approximately €280 per season. They spray it with the same rig they use for other organic treatments.

Sunburn losses: 30% → under 5% ~15% less irrigation water Organic certified ✓ ~€280 per season for 6 ha

We are ordering kaolin this month. It is the single cheapest, most immediate intervention we have found. You can do it this season, with existing equipment, at essentially no capital cost. If you have a citrus grove in Spain and you aren't doing this yet, this is the place to start.

The water problem

Heat and water are not separate problems. They are the same problem with two faces.

When it's 40°C and dry, your trees need more water — but midday irrigation doesn't help as much as it seems. Water applied through drip emitters at noon evaporates before it reaches root depth. More importantly, a tree under severe heat stress can't absorb water efficiently regardless: the roots slow down along with everything else. You end up using more water for less benefit.

The research on this is consistent. Irrigate before sunrise to 8am, or after 9pm. During heat peaks above 38°C for two or more days, add a short supplemental cycle at 9–10am (before peak heat, when the tree can still absorb it). Don't irrigate midday. Don't apply fertiliser during heatwaves — salt stress in the root zone compounds heat stress.

We're also changing when we do not irrigate. In the periods between heatwaves, a small controlled water deficit — giving trees 60–70% of what they'd normally get during late fruit development — has been shown in Spanish trials to actually improve fruit quality and colour in Navel and Navelina oranges, without reducing yield. The trees are slightly stressed, but productively so. The key is to detect and suspend this deficit the moment a heat event begins.

Our Agrónic fertigation controller is capable of all of this. The gap is sensors — we need soil moisture probes that tell us what's actually happening underground rather than irrigating on a calendar. That's the next purchase.

Case Study 2 — The Net Farm
Valencia Province, Spain · Multi-year agronomic study · Published 2021

What happens when you put a red net over Valencia oranges

Researchers from the Universitat Politècnica de València conducted a multi-year study on mature Valencia orange trees under photoselective shade nets — a specific type of netting that doesn't just block light but changes its spectrum. Standard black shade netting reduces total light. Red and pearl photoselective nets reduce total light but shift the balance toward wavelengths that are more efficient for photosynthesis.

The study compared open-air trees against trees under 30–50% shading from red and pearl nets, with adjusted irrigation (the shaded trees needed less water, which the researchers accounted for). Over multiple seasons, the net-covered trees produced significantly higher average yields than the open-air controls. Fruit was better coloured and had fewer defects. The trees under nets maintained active photosynthesis on days when the open-air trees had essentially shut down.

The mechanism isn't complicated. The net intercepts direct solar radiation before it reaches the leaf. The leaf stays 6–10°C cooler. The stomata stay open. The tree keeps feeding itself. The fruit doesn't sunburn.

The limitation is cost: permanent net infrastructure over a 4.5 hectare grove is a capital investment — posts, tensioning systems, and the nets themselves — in the range of €20,000–50,000 depending on the structure type. It is not a this-season solution. It is a multi-year investment that becomes more urgent every year the summers break records.

Higher yield vs open-air controls Better fruit colour and fewer defects 6–10°C cooler leaf surface 30–50% shade, red or pearl net
Case Study 3 — The Solar Orchard
Calabria, Italy · Azienda agricola · Ongoing

When solar panels become the shade structure

In Calabria — the toe of the Italian boot, with a climate almost identical to the Valencia coast — one citrus farm took the shade-net logic to its logical extension: instead of nets, they installed photovoltaic solar panels above the orchard canopy. The panels provide partial shade (roughly 20–30% coverage), generate electricity, and earn income from grid export while the trees work below them.

The farm reported three things that surprised them. First: fruit under the panels was larger and better coloured than fruit in the open sections, because the shade reduced heat stress during the critical summer months. Second: they needed to irrigate manually 70% less often during the summer peak — the shade reduced soil evaporation dramatically. Third: the electricity income paid back a significant portion of the installation cost within the first few years.

This approach — called agrivoltaics — is expanding rapidly across southern Europe. A 2025 European review of agrivoltaics for fruit crops found that 20–30% panel coverage is the sweet spot for citrus: enough shade to meaningfully reduce heat stress, not so much that it suppresses fruit sugar development and colour. Above 50% coverage, yield and quality begin to drop noticeably.

In Spain, the regulatory picture is still developing. Valencia has been cautious about solar installations on agricultural land. For now, we're watching this closely but treating it as a 3–5 year option rather than an immediate one. It's the right idea. The permissions aren't there yet.

Larger fruit, better colour 70% less manual summer irrigation Electricity income offsets cost 20–30% panel coverage is the sweet spot
Under the trees — the soil

The cheapest intervention available to any citrus grower is also the most underused: put something on the ground under your trees.

On a bare soil surface at 40°C in direct sun, the top few centimetres reach 55–60°C. Mulch that surface with 10 centimetres of shredded wood chips or your own pruning residues, and the soil temperature drops 8–15°C. The roots stay active. The soil retains moisture between irrigation cycles. Evaporation from the soil surface falls by 30–40%. The mulch decomposes slowly, feeding soil biology. You are doing four things at once for essentially nothing, if you're using your own pruning waste.

"Mulch is not glamorous. It is not a technology. But it is the closest thing to a universal win we found in this entire research process."

The only rule: keep mulch 15–20 centimetres back from the trunk. Wet mulch against a citrus trunk invites Phytophthora collar rot, which kills trees. Ring your mulch, don't pile it.

Below the mulch, there is an argument for biochar — a porous charcoal material produced by heating organic matter without oxygen. Biochar does not decompose like ordinary compost. It persists in the soil for hundreds of years, acting like a microscopic sponge: absorbing water when it's available, releasing it slowly when the soil dries. A 2025 study specifically on Mediterranean dryland farming confirmed that biochar-compost combinations can increase soil water-holding capacity by 15–30% in the sandy-loam soils common across this region.

At grove scale, the quantities are meaningful — 10–20 tonnes per hectare, incorporated to 20 centimetres depth. That is not a one-weekend project. But it is a one-time investment; biochar doesn't need replacing. We are planning to phase it in over two seasons starting this autumn.

Case Study 4 — Capturing rain before summer begins
Provence, France · Permaculture farm · Adapted for Mediterranean systems

How a French farm stopped losing its winter rainfall

A small mixed farm in Provence — similar climate, similar summer drought pressure — realised that their biggest water resource wasn't their irrigation allocation: it was the rain that fell during winter and spring, ran straight off their sloped fields, and was gone by April. They were sitting on a six-month supply of water that they were sending downhill every year.

They dug swales — shallow level channels cut exactly on contour across their fields. A swale is not a drainage ditch; it slopes not at all. Water that enters it stops moving. It sits, and slowly seeps into the ground. The root zone of the adjacent trees fills up. By the time summer arrives, the subsoil holds several weeks' worth of additional moisture that would otherwise have been lost.

They paired this with a keyline design — a whole-property water management strategy that routes water laterally across the land toward its drier areas, rather than allowing it to concentrate in valleys. The principle was developed by an Australian farmer named P.A. Yeomans in the 1950s, but it translates directly to European Mediterranean hillsides. A 2022 EU REGENERATE project published a specific guide for Mediterranean agrosilvopastoral systems using keyline methods.

The farm's water bill fell by 35% over three seasons. In the worst drought year on record for the region, their trees were visibly healthier than their neighbours'. The capital cost: one day with a hired tractor and a local permaculture consultant for the survey. Total: under €3,000 for the full property.

35% reduction in irrigation bill Subsoil moisture retained through summer Under €3,000 for full property Works on low-gradient land too
The house

We live here. That changes the calculus.

A citrus tree can lose a third of its fruit to heat and survive. A family with a baby cannot manage heat the same way. During the worst days this summer — 43°C by mid-afternoon — the inside of the farmhouse was hitting 36°C by 4pm. Our daughter's bedroom.

The research on infant heat risk is unambiguous. Spain's national health authority lists children under four as the highest-risk group for heat illness, alongside those over 75. The threshold for safe sleeping temperatures for infants is well below what we were experiencing.

Here is what we have learned about keeping an old Mediterranean farmhouse bearable without air conditioning — and some of what we have already done.

The single most effective measure is also the cheapest. External shutters or roller blinds on south and west-facing windows. Not internal blinds — those trap heat between the glass and the blind and bake the room. External shutters intercept solar radiation before it enters the glass. The difference in a room with external shutters kept closed from 10am to 7pm, versus the same room with internal blinds, is 4–6°C. We fitted exterior roller blinds on the west-facing bedroom windows last month. This made an immediate, noticeable difference.

Traditional Valencian farmhouse design · Historical precedent

What the people who built these houses already knew

Traditional Valencian agricultural houses — the masías and cortijos of the Ribera Alta — were not designed by climate scientists. They were designed by people who lived through July here for centuries, without mechanical cooling, and needed to survive it.

What they built: thick stone or adobe walls (40–60 centimetres, sometimes more) that absorb heat during the day and release it slowly overnight. Small windows, deep-set into the wall. External shutters — persianillas — closed against the afternoon sun. Interior courtyards that channelled shade and created stack-effect ventilation. Trees planted on the south and west sides of the building, deciduous ones specifically so they'd be bare in winter and let the sun in.

They knew what we're rediscovering through research: the building should be closed and dark during the day, then opened wide at night to flush the accumulated heat with cool air. In Carcaixent, even on the hottest days, temperatures drop 10–12°C after midnight. A house that is properly sealed during the day and opened properly at night maintains indoor temperatures 8–10°C below the outdoor peak without any mechanical help.

We have stopped thinking of this as "no air conditioning" — a deprivation. We have started thinking of it as operating the building correctly. The building was designed for this. We were just ignoring its design logic.

8–10°C below outdoor peak (passive only) Close during the day, open fully at night External shutters are 6–10× more effective than internal blinds Zero cost once installed

The other thing that works, specifically for inland Valencia's dry summer climate, is evaporative cooling. At 20–30% relative humidity — which is common here in July — a simple evaporative cooler (a box that pulls air through a wet pad) can cool a room by 8–12°C using a fraction of the electricity of a standard air conditioner. They cost €150–400 at any hardware shop. They don't work well when it's humid — during Levante wind events from the sea, turn them off. But for dry inland heat, they are effective and cheap.

For the infant specifically: we keep her in the coolest interior room between noon and 8pm. We dress her in loose cotton. We increase feeding frequency — the body's hydration demands are higher in heat. We have a Xiaomi sensor in her room that alerts our phones when temperature exceeds 28°C. We monitor the Ministerio de Sanidad's Meteosalud map every morning during heat alerts. These are not dramatic measures. They are attentive ones.

What we're doing — in order

There is more to do here than can be done in a season. So we are being deliberate about sequence: immediate actions first, capital investments planned ahead, systemic changes over the next two to three years.

  • 1Kaolin clay this season. Ordering now. Apply from late May every 3–4 weeks through October. This is the fastest return for the least cost. Sunburn protection, heat reduction, organic pest deterrence — in one spray.
  • 2Mulch under all 1,100 trees this autumn. Using our own pruning residues. 10 centimetres depth, trunk cleared by 15 centimetres. This is a significant amount of material to distribute but uses what we already produce. Immediate impact on root-zone temperature and moisture retention.
  • 3Agrónic reprogramming + soil moisture sensors. Shift all irrigation to early morning and evening scheduling. Add AgroBee-L wireless soil moisture probes (native to our controller) at four locations across the grove. Stop guessing, start measuring. Target: 20–30% reduction in water use with better tree health outcomes.
  • 4Dwelling: external shutters, vines, and evaporative cooler. Finish fitting exterior shutters on south and west windows. Plant grapevines and fig against the south and west walls on trellis (2–3 year establishment — do it this autumn so they're working by summer 2028). Portable evaporative cooler for sleeping areas on extreme heat days.
  • 5Apply for GVA grants in spring 2027. The Conselleria de Agricultura offers up to 80% of organic certification costs (procedure 18688) and up to 60% of capital investments in shade infrastructure and soil amendments (procedure 574, the PAC modernisation grant). A shade netting project for the highest-value blocks, biochar incorporation, and sensor upgrades could realistically qualify for €20,000–40,000 in grant support. We are preparing the application now.
The funding landscape

Organic certification aid (GVA, annual): Up to 80% of CAECV certification costs, maximum €2,000/year. Apply via sede.gva.es, procedure 18688. Window opens approximately May each year. Once you qualify, certification costs you roughly €400/year out of pocket.

PAC Modernisation Grant (GVA, PAC 2023–2027): €50 million budget for 2026. Eligible for shade infrastructure, irrigation upgrades, soil amendments, and on-farm climate adaptation. Typically 40–60% of eligible costs. Apply via procedure 574. Engage AVAASAJA (avaasaja.org) for application support — they provide free guidance to Valencia farmers.

PAC Eco-schemes (annual DUN declaration): Additional per-hectare payments for certified organic production (€350–600/ha/year) and for maintaining inter-row cover crops in fruit orchards. Declare in your annual PAC DUN filing — no separate application required.

IVACE energy grants: Up to 80% subsidy for on-farm solar self-consumption. Relevant when agrivoltaics become a serious option.

What we don't know yet

We are new farmers in an unprecedented climate. There are things we are still learning.

We don't know how our specific varieties — Clemenules and Navelina — will perform under the new heat regime over the next decade. The rootstocks they're grafted onto are a factor too. If we replant, we now know to use Carrizo citrange (confirmed more heat-tolerant than the alternatives) or the IVIA Forner-Alcaide series, bred specifically for Valencia conditions. But our oldest trees were planted decades before any of this research existed.

We don't know if a single wet winter — like the one that followed the DANA storm in 2024 — can meaningfully replenish the soil moisture that a long dry summer exhausts. We are implementing the swales and water harvesting earthworks partly as a bet that it can.

And we don't know what 2027 will bring. The trend is one direction. The question isn't whether to adapt. It's how fast, and with what resources.

We are a small farm on an 80-million-year-old limestone hillside. The rock has seen worse than this. We are trying to think at its pace, not our own.
