How the pines got here

A 1950s decision we are still living with.

In the 1950s, Francisco Franco's regime launched the Patrimonio Forestal del Estado — one of the largest reforestation programmes in European history. The goal was legitimate: decades of deforestation had left Spanish hillsides bare and eroding. Over three billion trees were planted across the country.

On this hillside, the programme chose Aleppo pine. It grows fast in thin soils, which made it effective at stopping erosion quickly. What it could not do was belong here. This limestone slope is holm oak territory — a species with deep roots, low fire risk, and a relationship with the soil that Aleppo pine never built. The pine displaced what was already beginning to grow back naturally.

Seventy years later, the trees are mature, resinous, and highly flammable — sitting directly above orange groves that took decades to establish. The holm oak has been pushing back slowly in the gaps where the pine canopy opens. We are accelerating that process: clearing pine section by section, planting Quercus ilex, and letting the hillside return to what it was always trying to become.

The switch

Pine vs Oak — why it matters.

Going out
Aleppo Pine
Pinus halepensis

Aleppo pine moved in after the original holm oak was cleared. It grows fast and burns easily — resin-soaked wood, shallow roots, no soil benefit. A placeholder, not a permanent species. Above orange groves, it is a fire risk we cannot afford.

VS
Coming in
Holm Oak
Quercus ilex

Quercus ilex is the climax species of this limestone hillside — the tree this land has been trying to grow for thousands of years. Deep taproot through karst. Leathery leaves that resist drought and fire. Soil carbon the pine never built. It does not need managing. It needs planting.

🔥 Fire risk Low
🌱 Native to limestone Yes — climax species
🌿 Root depth Deep
💨 Carbon storage High — wood + soil

Underground

Where the difference really is.

Aleppo pine roots stay in the topsoil — shallow, lateral, gone at 40 cm. They hold the surface but do nothing below it. Holm oak drives a single taproot straight through the subsoil and into the limestone, following cracks to water three metres down. That root is what builds drought resilience, carbon depth, and soil structure that outlasts the tree itself.
