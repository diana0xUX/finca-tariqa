The Geology

Before the grove, this was a warm shallow sea.

The limestone beneath Finca Tariqa formed 80 million years ago from the compressed shells of marine creatures. This is the rock the oranges grow from.

The Story

Seabed to hillside. 80 million years.

When the tectonic forces that built the Alps began compressing Iberia, this ancient seabed was squeezed, folded, and pushed upward. The fractures that now run through it hold the water that feeds the farm's karst well, 41 metres below the grove.

The limestone dissolves slowly, releasing calcium and trace minerals into the shallow soil. No alluvial valley soil can replicate this mineral profile — and minerals are a primary driver of flavour in citrus, as they are in Burgundy or Priorat wine.

The hillside sits above the Júcar valley flood plain. When the DANA storm dropped 500mm of rain in 8 hours in October 2024, the valley flooded catastrophically. The limestone shed water through its fractures and recharged the aquifer. Height is structural resilience.

What limestone does

For the fruit. For the water. For the future.

For the fruit

Thin soil and fast drainage create natural water deficit — the same stress winemakers chase. The Brix rises. The aromatics deepen. Minerals dissolved from 80 million years of limestone enter the root system and show up in the flavour profile of the fruit.

For the water

The karst aquifer beneath the grove is fed by rainfall on the limestone massif. It sits on its own geological formation, structurally separate from the Júcar valley floor water system — and at 40km inland, well beyond the reach of coastal salinisation. An advantage that grows more valuable as Mediterranean water competition intensifies.

For the future

A hillside with its own water, above the flood plain, on limestone terrain — in a warming climate, that is a different risk category from valley floor farming. The geology is the resilience strategy. It was there before the farm. It will be there after.
