The Science

Why limestone changes everything.

♡

Mineral profile

Cretaceous limestone, dissolved slowly over 80 million years, feeds the trees minerals that valley alluvial soil cannot replicate. The karst aquifer beneath Puig Gros means water passes through ancient rock before it reaches the roots.

○

Natural deficit

Thin rendzina soil, fast drainage, Mediterranean sun — the tree experiences mild water stress every summer. Brix (sugar concentration) rises. Valley fruit cannot match it. The same mechanism that winemakers in Burgundy and Priorat deliberately engineer — here, the geology provides it for free.

◆

Terpene spike

Under stress, citrus redirects energy into secondary aromatic compounds — limonene, aromatic aldehydes. The chemistry of perfume. Water-deficit citrus carries measurably deeper aromatics than irrigated valley fruit.

Three Varieties

Each one chosen for when it ripens.

Oct – Jan

Navelina

First of the season. Sweet from the start.

The first orange of the new season, ready in October when most groves are still quiet. Sweet, easy to peel, reliably high Brix on this hillside — the limestone terroir does what warmer valley soils cannot.

The Navelina trees on the lower terraces took the brunt of the October 2024 DANA storm. They are being worked through systematically — diagnosed tree by tree, managed back to full health. The second season is about acting on what was found.

Feb – May

Navel Lane Late

The late one. Worth waiting for.

Arrives in February and runs into May — alone in a market that most navels have already left. The longest-keeping navel variety, with five consecutive seasons of growing volume on this land. Planted on the upper terraces where drainage is sharpest.

Agronomic research on warming Mediterranean climates identifies late navels as strategically well-positioned: their harvest window shifts later as winters shorten, into space that earlier varieties cannot fill. This hillside is positioned for it now.

May – Jul

Valencia Late

The summer orange. Rare on the market.

The true summer orange — still ripening in June and July when every other variety has finished. Valencia Late is uncommon in Spanish groves precisely because it demands patience: the tree holds fruit through the heat, and only rewards you in the market window when competition has almost disappeared.

It completes the nine-month harvest calendar. From the first Navelina in October to the last Valencia Late in July — a single hillside, three varieties, fresh fruit every month of the year except August and September.

Going Organic

Converting the grove — one season at a time.

The grove is in transition to certified organic production under CAECV, the Valencian organic certification authority. The three-year conversion period, required under EU Regulation 2018/848 for perennial crops, begins when we register — which we are doing now.

From the second year of conversion, the fruit can carry the "en conversión a ecológico" label — a real quality signal, with a documented price premium in cooperative and direct channels. Full organic certification follows in the third year. Every input, every application, every soil reading is logged in a field diary from day one. The records are the proof.

The trees are old enough that the limestone terroir has been doing the work for decades. The certification makes it legible to the market.
